# Problem 6: 2D Electric Field Mapping and Equilibrium Analysis

This task involves the numerical simulation and visualization of the electric field generated by a system of three point charges in a two-dimensional plane. We explore the superposition principle, numerical root-finding for equilibrium points, and the stability of those points.

---

### 1. Calculation of the Field Vector $\vec{E}(x, y)$

The electric field $\vec{E}$ at any point $(x, y)$ is the vector sum of the fields produced by each individual charge (Superposition Principle). For a single charge $q_i$ located at $(x_i, y_i)$, the field at $(x, y)$ is:

$$\vec{E}_i = k \frac{q_i}{r_i^2} \hat{r}_i = k \frac{q_i}{r_i^3} \vec{r}_i$$

Where $r_i = \sqrt{(x-x_i)^2 + (y-y_i)^2}$. The total field is:
$$\vec{E}_{total}(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{3} \vec{E}_i$$

---

### 2. Numerical Search for Equilibrium Points

An equilibrium point exists where the net electric field is zero:
$$\vec{E}_{total}(x, y) = 0$$

Since the equation is non-linear, we find these points numerically using a **Gradient Descent** or **Newton-Raphson** approach on the magnitude of the field $|E|$. These points represent locations where a test charge would experience no net force.

---

### 3. Stability Investigation

To determine if an equilibrium point is stable or unstable, we examine the behavior of the field during a **small displacement** ($\Delta x, \Delta y$).
* **Stable:** If the field vectors point back toward the equilibrium point (restoring force).
* **Unstable/Saddle:** If the field vectors point away or vary in different directions.
* **Earnshaw’s Theorem:** Mathematically, we prove that a point charge cannot be maintained in a stable stationary equilibrium by electrostatic forces alone.

---

### 4. Comparative Analysis: Two vs. Three Charges

| Configuration | Field Geometry | Equilibrium |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Two Charges** | Simple dipole or repulsion line. | One point on the axis between them. |
| **Three Charges** | Complex interference patterns. | Multiple points, often forming a "null zone" in the center of the configuration. |
